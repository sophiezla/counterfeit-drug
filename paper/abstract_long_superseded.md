In binary image tasks that ask whether something is genuine — authentic or counterfeit packaging, real or generated imagery, unaltered or forged documents — one class is almost always harder to obtain than the other. The scarce class is therefore sourced by a different procedure: screen-captured instead of photographed, generated instead of collected, edited instead of found. We argue that this asymmetry makes *class-conditional provenance confounding* the default outcome of dataset construction rather than an occasional accident, and that it is invisible to every in-distribution evaluation, because a held-out partition drawn from the same pool inherits the confound exactly. We characterise the failure, give a cheap detector for it, measure what it costs when undetected, and show how much of the damage a label-free correction repairs.

The case study is a public counterfeit-medicine dataset (Kaggle *Fake vs Real Medicine*, 661 images), on data of a kind for which accuracies in the low-to-mid 90s are routinely reported. Its two classes were acquired by two different pipelines with no exceptions: every counterfeit-labelled file is a screen capture stored as PNG and every authentic-labelled file is a downloaded photograph stored as JPEG (238/238 and 272/272 after filtering; 240/240 and 421/421 as shipped). The archive additionally ships a train/validation/test partition whose training folder contains all 661 images and whose validation and test folders are proper subsets of it, so any study adopting the split as distributed trains on 100% of its own evaluation data. The two groups differ in mean brightness (0.555 vs. 0.767 on a 0–1 scale; two-sample *t* = 17.0, *p* ≈ 0), in median short-side resolution (405 px vs. 223 px) and in mean file size (339 kB vs. 6.0 kB, a 56× gap). A 96-dimensional colour histogram with logistic regression reaches 83.8% accuracy on a leakage-free split of this pool, and an exact Shapley-value decomposition of that model shows its decision is carried almost entirely by three features — the top intensity bin of each RGB channel (mean |φ| = 0.079–0.082, versus ≤ 0.002 for all 93 remaining features). A metadata-only oracle bounds the effect exactly: a logistic regression on three acquisition scalars and no pixels reaches 100% on the leakage-free test partition, encoded file size alone suffices, and the filename extension classifies all 510 images correctly by construction — so the accuracy ceiling attributable to acquisition alone on this dataset is 1.000.

We quantify the consequences on four model families (colour histogram + logistic regression; a 23,938-parameter CNN with a global-average-pooling head; frozen MobileNetV3-Small; frozen EfficientNet-B0) under three evaluation protocols: a naive image-level split, a product-identity-grouped leakage-free split with 5-fold grouped cross-validation, and an external split of 150 authentic packaging photographs from an independent source (Mendeley, verified non-duplicative by rotation-aware perceptual hashing). Correcting the split protocol changes in-distribution accuracy by at most 6.8 points, and no pairwise McNemar's test between the four models is significant (all *p* ≥ 0.118). Evaluating on the external set is far more informative: before correction, the two weakest models classify 0/150 external authentic photographs correctly and EfficientNet-B0 manages 3.3%, despite 84–97% in-distribution accuracy on the authentic class. Grad-CAM on those external images shows confident (*p* = 0.94–1.00), spatially sensible attention on the printed product name — a decision calibrated to the wrong cue rather than a random failure.

A label-free, deployable three-stage normalisation applied identically to every partition — a 128 px short-side resolution bottleneck, mean-brightness rescaling to a fixed target, and a fixed-quality JPEG re-encode — raises external authentic accuracy from 0.0% to 86.0% (small CNN), from 69.3% to 77.3% (MobileNetV3-Small) and from 3.3% to 80.7% (EfficientNet-B0), while in-distribution test accuracy moves only between −1.4 and 0.0 points. A second external distribution — the same 150 products rephotographed on different hardware under a different lighting protocol — shows that this repair is not uniform: the two frozen pretrained backbones hold (0.773 → 0.725 and 0.807 → 0.832, both within sampling noise) while the from-scratch CNN collapses from 0.860 to 0.463. A complete attention audit over all 62 heatmaps then constrains what the surviving accuracy means: on external images both backbones take their evidence for "authentic" from the background and their evidence for "counterfeit" from the product, without exception in 40 maps, and both external sets share one backdrop. We therefore report that the correction transfers across a change of camera for pretrained features and not for a small network trained from scratch, and we do not claim that any model here recognises packaging. Ablating the three axes on EfficientNet-B0 in a single run gives 5.3% (none) → 12.7% (compression) → 50.7% (resolution + brightness) → 78.0% (all three), i.e. the axes are strongly complementary; white-balance normalisation was tested and ruled out. The colour-histogram baseline gains nothing and loses its in-distribution accuracy (83.8% → 54.1%), confirming it had no signal beyond the shortcut. A synthetic counterfeit-proxy stress test, constructed by perturbing the same external photographs, shows that no model achieves well-calibrated counterfeit recall out of distribution (best ROC-AUC 0.895, attained by the model whose decision threshold is simultaneously so miscalibrated that its accuracy is pinned at exactly 0.500).

The detector we propose is a *provenance audit*: fit the classifier you intend to use to acquisition metadata alone — format, encoded size, resolution, aspect ratio — under your own leakage-free split, and read its accuracy as a lower bound on how much of any pixel-based result the acquisition process can explain. It costs seconds and needs no external data. On the case-study dataset it returns 1.000, and even aspect ratio alone returns 0.803. Run against a second, independently published dataset it returns only 0.717, although that dataset is confounded at least as severely — its publisher had resized every image to a common resolution and format before release, which destroyed the acquisition traces without removing the confound. The audit is therefore a necessary but not sufficient screen, and we pair it with the content and near-duplicate checks that did catch the second case.

We conclude that provenance confounding should be treated as the expected consequence of asymmetric class sourcing rather than as a defect of particular datasets; that a metadata audit and an externally acquired test set, not a larger architecture, are what distinguish the intended task from its shortcut; and that a substantial part of the resulting generalisation failure is a correctable preprocessing artefact rather than an inherent limit of the task. The same format/resolution/compression signature we document has been independently reported in generated-image detection, where real images are lossy web JPEGs and generated images are lossless PNGs, and where a detector trained on the standard benchmark has been shown to behave partly as a JPEG detector [30] — arrived at by a completely different route, which is what a structural cause rather than a coincidence predicts.
